# Retarget the metric, then rebuild the enumeration ladder

**85–90 per cent is unattainable on the metric currently reported and comfortably attainable on a different one, and the honest move is to report both.** Strict per-case recall against the 687 clause-citing tests of ISO/IEC TS 18013-6 cannot plausibly reach 85 per cent: the highest figure anywhere in the literature for recall against a human-written high-level test suite is **0.84**, obtained under a permissive cosine-similarity matcher on a single Bluetooth document with only **90 reference cases**, while the same method scored **0.37** on a sister dataset and **0.02** zero-shot ([arXiv 2510.03641](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03641v1)). Against 687 references, under a same-object/same-condition/same-outcome criterion, a defensible engineering ceiling is roughly 35–60 per cent — and the arithmetic converges on that band from an entirely independent direction: **257 type/encoding checks plus 44 behaviour/flow checks, 301 of 687 or ~44 per cent of the clause-citing suite, belong to ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-4 techniques the generator does not implement at all** ([29119-4:2021 preview](https://assets.vde-verlag.de/iec-normen/preview-pdf/info_isoiecieee29119-4%7Bed2.0%7Den.pdf)). Where 85–90 per cent *is* defensible is on the genre's own native metric: coverage of the input standard's normative requirements by at least one test purpose, which is what ISO/IEC 9646-2 clause 10.3.5 demands, what ETSI's ETR 266 Guidance 5 operationalises as "any mandatory or optional item of the ICS is covered by at least one TP", and what 29119-4 clause 6.2.12 calls requirements-based testing coverage. The mechanisms below are ranked by how much they move the strict number, every one is drawn from a published source, and the two that are *not* generic across RFCs, OpenID specifications and ISO deliverables are the two that matter most — which is the finding that should shape the tool's roadmap.

## The two numbers: 13.8 per cent is the hard one, and it is measured against the wrong denominator

The first thing to fix costs nothing in generator engineering and is a correctness fix rather than a presentational one. Three distinct denominators exist in the official suite and all three should be stated: **907 total cases; 687 citing a clause of the standard under test; and the in-edition subset of those 687**. The 220 cases that cite no clause of ISO/IEC 18013-5 are, by the suite's own `Reference` field, grounded in external specifications — they test requirements that are not in the input document at all and are unanswerable by construction unless those specifications are also supplied. The 687 themselves were written against the published 2021 edition while the measurement input is the 2020 DIS draft, and the genre makes that stratification mechanical rather than inferential: in the ISO/IEC 9646-2 / TPLan structure the requirement reference is a **first-class header field**, not prose ([ETSI CTI TPLan page](https://portal.etsi.org/CTI/ApproachToTesting/TestLanguages/TPLAN.htm)), so each official test's cited clause can be resolved in both editions and classified *unchanged / reworded / changed / absent*. Only the first two belong in a scored denominator. This single change is the largest honest improvement available, and no publication covers it — there is no retrieved work at all on specification-edition mismatch between an input document and a reference suite.

The second thing to fix is that part of the 13.8 per cent is measurement loss, not generation loss. The current protocol — top-5 lexical retrieval by weighted token overlap, then an LLM equivalence judgment — stacks four named threats. Text-only information retrieval between test artefacts achieves **average recall below 40 per cent and precision below 30 per cent**, with vocabulary mismatch the identified cause, while a merged matrix taking the union of four techniques reached **93 per cent recall** on the same data ([SBES](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbes/article/view/17022)); structural signals beat text similarity outright, at **above 90 per cent precision and recall** when requirements were linked to tests through realised features rather than prose ([ASE 2011](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/conferences/ASE/ase2011/www.continuinged.ku.edu/programs/ase/papers/26.html)). On the judge side, the best SE meta-evaluation reports judge–human Pearson of **81.3 on code translation, 68.5 on code generation and 26.2 on code summarisation** against human inter-rater Pearson of **85.9 / 79.7 / 73.7**, finds pairwise comparison *worse* than individual scoring with order-swap consistency of only **11–24**, and recommends validating the judge per task before relying on it ([arXiv 2502.06193v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06193v3)). Prompt-level interventions alone are large enough to change task-level conclusions and alter model rankings ([arXiv 2604.16790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16790)). A "does any of these five verify the same check?" prompt is a five-way comparison in the exact regime those results condemn, and the fix is cheap: decide system-under-test, check type and applicability class structurally outside the judge, judge one candidate at a time and aggregate by OR, report the score at two values of *k*, and run a second judge from a different model family. The direction of the remaining bias is arguable and should be argued: self-preference inflates scores, so a same-family judge is **conservative** at 13.8 per cent and **generous** on the 56/50/54-of-60 quality ratings.

Here the paper has an advantage no published system has. Every recall figure in the extraction literature is measured against ground truth the authors wrote themselves — ParCleanse's hand-written formats cost **0.5 to 6 hours per protocol** ([arXiv 2504.18050](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18050v1)). The 687 official tests are an externally authored oracle, so 13.8 per cent is not a weaker version of ParCleanse's 95 per cent; it is a different and stricter kind of number, and the first one reported at scale.

## Forty-four per cent of the official suite belongs to techniques the generator never runs

The clearest explanation of 13.8 per cent is not model quality. It is that **ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-4:2021 defines twelve specification-based test design techniques, each paired one-to-one with its own coverage-measurement clause, and the implemented ladder is a hand-rolled fusion of two of them.** The verified clause list runs equivalence partitioning (5.2.1), classification tree method (5.2.2), boundary value analysis (5.2.3), **syntax testing (5.2.4)**, **combinatorial test design (5.2.5)**, **decision table testing (5.2.6)**, cause-effect graphing (5.2.7), **state transition testing (5.2.8)**, **scenario testing (5.2.9)**, random testing (5.2.10), metamorphic testing (5.2.11) and **requirements-based testing (5.2.12)** ([29119-4:2021 preview](https://assets.vde-verlag.de/iec-normen/preview-pdf/info_isoiecieee29119-4%7Bed2.0%7Den.pdf)). Mapping the official suite's check families onto them is unambiguous: the 257 type/encoding well-formedness checks are syntax testing, for which the standard even defines **Backus-Naur Form** as a term; the 44 behaviour/protocol-flow checks are state transition and scenario testing; the Technology and Profile applicability tags that gate every case are combinatorial test design plus ICS selection; and conditional presence is decision table territory, where the current `condition true` / `condition false` rungs are a degenerate two-case approximation of a table that over *k* conditions yields up to 2^*k* rules before infeasible-rule elimination.

That arithmetic sets a ceiling independent of any judge: at perfect execution within the implemented techniques, roughly **56 per cent of the 687 clause-citing tests** is the most the current ladder can reach. It also explains why adding rungs will not help. The most directly applicable published result is from an automotive network-controller study in which **a sixfold increase in the number of model-based tests produced an 11 per cent increase in detected errors**, while automatically generated suites detected as many errors as hand-crafted ones *of the same size derived from the same model* ([Pretschner et al., ICSE 2005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.06815)). Coverage comes from extending the model, not from enumerating harder over it.

The inflation side is equally well documented, and the standards bodies predicted it. The tool produces **3.84 test cases per extracted requirement** (3231/842); ETSI's IPv6 project, the one documented case of running this pipeline end-to-end over RFCs, produced **0.32 conformance tests per catalogued requirement** (1112 requirements → 356 validated conformance tests), with 695 → 105 for IPsec and 1098 → 163 for Mobile IPv6 ([Randall, ITU-T workshop 2006](https://itu.int/dms_pub/itu-t/oth/06/02/T06020000020001PDFE.pdf)) — a roughly twelvefold density difference in the opposite direction from intuition. ETSI's methodology standard sets a size norm of "**never exceed a few hundreds of tests (e.g. 400 tests) no matter how complex the protocol is**", states flatly that "**going from conformance requirements to TPs is an empirical job today; no metrics exist to assess the coverage of a test specification**", and warns in 1995 that "**the formal and automatic derivation of tests generally leads to a huge number of tests without interest**" ([ETS 300 406, Annex D.3](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_i_ets/300400_300499/300406/01_60/ets_300406e01p.pdf)). The pipeline as described has the inflating half of 9646-2 — one coverage item, one test case — and not the deflating half, which clause 10.3.3 prescribes as a formal second stage with supersession bookkeeping ([ITU-T X.291](https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-X.291-199504-I!!PDF-E&type=items)).

The top of that list is not where the intuition points, and the reason is structural. **An applicability predicate is not decoration; it is a type difference.** ETSI's rules make the ICS proforma a table whose rows are `{Item, feature, Ref. clause, Status ∈ {M, O, c*n*}, Support}` with an `Allowed values / Supported values` pair for numeric features, and they make it normative that "**all the roles, major capabilities, negotiation capabilities, timers, PDUs shall be subject to an ICS question, whatever their status may be**", with an equally mechanical factoring rule: where every parameter of a structure inherits its status from the structure, emit one question rather than enumerating the parameters ([ETS 300 406 Annex A.2](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_i_ets/300400_300499/300406/01_60/ets_300406e01p.pdf)). That row schema is a near-exact match for the "objects with attributes" the second extraction pass already produces — the pipeline builds something isomorphic to an ICS proforma without naming it. Naming it, emitting it and keying coverage items to ICS items aligns the tool with the genre's *actual* published coverage criterion, and the factoring rule is the deduplication heuristic whose absence plausibly explains 921 objects becoming 3257 coverage items. EG 203 130 clause 6.1 additionally mandates two per-requirement attributes the pipeline does not produce — **Role(s) to apply to** and **Testability** — and Role is exactly what TS 18013-6's Technology tag encodes. A requirement extractor that assigns no role cannot gate its items the way the official suite gates its tests.

Mechanism 4 deserves its unusual rank because it is the one change that alters *what gets written* rather than how much. The official suite's 907 cases decompose into **118 family headers, 112 distinct precondition blocks (the commonest shared by 154 cases), a handful of body patterns per check family, and 907 instantiations**; TPLan's own factorisation mechanisms are named — `Group`/`End Group` hierarchy and `#include` to "maintain a single source of vocabulary extensions" — and the notation's authors report that user-defined vocabulary "**initially grows quite rapidly … After that, however, the need for new definitions levels off quickly**" ([TPLan paper](https://dl.ifip.org/index.html/db/conf/pts/testcom2007/SchulzWR07.pdf)). The number of prose artefacts a model must write is therefore on the order of 100–250, not 3231. The 27 "perform all Common_X test cases" entries are the X.291 clause 11 generic-test-suite construct, and basic interconnection tests "**shall not involve any additional test purposes**" — a flat generator cannot represent those cases, let alone match them. Granularity follows the same logic: TPLan's rule is that "`when` and `then` **should not be misunderstood to require a complete specification of accurate test sequence(s)**", which is why the human cases run at ~2.4 steps and ~2.9 parallel expected results, and that should be a hard output schema rather than a model decision.

Mechanism 5 supplies the missing deflation stage with published counts. Base Choice Coverage costs **1 + Σ(B*ᵢ* − 1)** tests — linear in blocks, not multiplicative — which is "one nominal case plus one variation per deviating value" and is far closer to 7.7 cases per family than any cross product. For applicability tags specifically, NIST's covering arrays give the economics: on a realistic Android configuration space, **t=2 needs 29 tests (0.02 per cent of exhaustive) and t=3 needs 137**, with multiple studies showing fault detection equal to exhaustive testing at a **20× to 700× reduction** ([NIST ACTS](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/automated-combinatorial-testing-for-software)). The empirical interaction rule — **67 per cent of failures from a single parameter, 93 per cent at 2-way, 98 per cent at 3-way**, reaching 100 per cent at 4–6 way — justifies stopping at small *t*, tempered by NIST's own warning that "**pairwise testing may miss 10 per cent to 40 per cent or more of system bugs**" ([NIST SP 800-142](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-142.pdf)). The category-partition constraint ladder does the rest: `[property]`/`[if]` annotations took the canonical FIND specification from **1944 test frames to 678**, and `[error]`/`[single]` took it to **125** — a 15.6× cut. The current `encoding invalid` / `value invalid` / `boundary outside` rungs are precisely `[error]` choices that have been crossed with everything else, which is why item count inflates without coverage gain.

Mechanism 6 is where the published numbers are strongest, and they are about architecture rather than model size. ParCleanse's ablation shows **field-name recall falling from 95 to 54 per cent and independent-constraint recall from 82 to 26 per cent** when divide-and-conquer is removed, with dependent constraints going to zero; its traceability-assisted refinement alone lifts independent-constraint recall **68 → 82 per cent** ([arXiv 2504.18050](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18050v1)). L3X reports long-list recall of **38.7 per cent zero-shot single-prompt rising to 83.6–85.2 per cent** with decomposition against an oracle ceiling of 88, and a clean union curve of **76.2 → 81.1 → 83.6** for one, three and five query reformulations ([L3X, TACL](https://people.mpi-inf.mpg.de/~ssinghan/tacl_paper.pdf)). Two cautions follow. First, **union, do not vote**: SpecGPT's five-model majority vote bought 11.5 precision points at a cost of **2.1 recall points** versus its best single model ([arXiv 2510.14348](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14348v1)) — the wrong trade for a recall-limited pipeline whose rule-based verifier and verbatim-quote requirement can play the precision role instead. Second, **exhaust decomposition before upgrading the model**: L3X's own 70B scored 38.7–47.7 per cent *before* decomposition, and SpecGPT's direct prompting without designed prompts scored **F1 14.87 against 91.14** with them. The 31B local model sits between L3X's 8B (<50 per cent recall) and 70B (~80 per cent) points, so size is plausibly contributory but is demonstrably not the binding constraint.

Expectations should stay calibrated even with all nine in place. Constraint extraction is the permanent weak spot across every system measured — **82 per cent for independent and 44 per cent for dependent constraints at best, 16.7 per cent and 0 per cent at worst** ([ParCleanse](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18050v1); [AUTOSPEC](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17977v1)) — and state machines are worse still, with **67 per cent (TCP) and 96 per cent (DCCP) of residual FSM errors attributed to information missing from or ambiguous in the document text** ([Pacheco et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.09470)). On fault exposure, the best open model reaches **43.8 per cent against 93.3 per cent for human experts** ([ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-short.82.pdf)), and coverage-directed generation on industrial control software matched manual code coverage at ~90 per cent time saving while **manual tests still caught logical, timer and negation faults the generated suite missed** ([Enoiu et al.](https://www.es.mdu.se/pdf_publications/4576.pdf)). A residue of the human suite will remain unmatched by design, and 9646-2 clause 10.3.6 says to record it: test purposes for untestable requirements exist precisely "**by indicating gaps in the ATSs**" — the published precedent for the tool's 66 recorded gaps, which are standard-mandated behaviour rather than a workaround.

## Genericity turns on two inputs, and only one of them generalises cleanly

Almost everything above is standard-agnostic by its own authors' claim. 29119-4 "aims to provide stakeholders with the ability to design test cases for software testing in **any organization**" and selects techniques by ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristic rather than by domain; ISO/IEC 9646 "is widely used by **protocol and profile specifiers**" generally; TPLan was designed so that "the core notation [is] as independent as possible from any specific application area", opening it "**from, for example, telecommunication to civil engineering**", and has been applied to an IETF RFC (RFC 2460) and an ETSI radio standard with the same notation; the classification tree method "works on **any type of system under test**"; and NIST's covering arrays are configuration-agnostic. ETSI applies the identical TSS&TP pipeline to XML and JSON Schema conformance in TS 103 254 as to protocols. Mechanisms 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 therefore transfer on their own terms.

Two inputs do not transfer for free. The first is the **encoding grammar** that syntax testing requires, and it is the single largest genericity caveat because that technique covers 257 of 687 official tests. Here the standards-families diverge sharply in the tool's favour and against it. An RFC usually carries ABNF, and IETF runs a production extractor for it — Bill's ABNF Parser behind the Author Tools service — though its documented limit is instructive: the output "**is a concatenation of all the ABNF fragments in the document, including the appendix. Therefore results may include duplicates and invalid ABNF syntax**" ([author-tools.ietf.org/abnf](https://author-tools.ietf.org/abnf)). YANG has `rfcstrip` and `xym`, both marker-driven, and a YANG Doctors review notes that without the filename after `<CODE BEGINS>` "**the tool 'rfcstrip' will not extract the correct file**" ([datatracker review](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/review-ietf-netmod-rfc8407bis-11-yangdoctors-lc-liu-2024-06-17/00/)). An OpenID specification carries JSON Schema or JSON object tables, and `fences` generates valid and invalid samples from a schema "**systematically instead of randomly**" ([PyPI: fences](https://pypi.org/project/fences/)). A 3GPP deliverable carries ASN.1, extracted in production by Berserker from the Word source and earlier by OpenAirInterface ([arXiv 2107.01912](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.01912)). An ISO deliverable carries tables, and sometimes CDDL — for which **no extractor exists at all**, only validators such as cddl-rs. So the honest genericity statement is: syntax testing works on an RFC and an OpenID specification today, works on 3GPP with an existing extractor, and on ISO depends on whether the document embeds a grammar; where it does not, the 37-per-cent slice is simply unavailable and must be declared missing rather than approximated.

The second non-transferring input is the **classification or aspect selection** itself, and the literature is unanimous that this, not enumeration, is the bottleneck. NIST warns that "**input space modeling can be a significant undertaking, and needs to be carefully managed**"; CTM's documented weakness is that "**no algorithm guides the selection of aspects**"; and EG 203 130 catalogues what defeats mechanical derivation from prose — ambiguity, untestable-but-marked-testable requirements, incompleteness, inconsistencies, and "lack of reasonable integrity between different parts of the standard" ([EG 203 130 §6.2](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_eg/203100_203199/203130/01.01.01_60/eg_203130v010101p.pdf)). Mechanism 2's requirement-catalogue stage is the part of this that *is* prescribed in enough detail to mechanise, and the prescription matches the tool's existing behaviour almost item for item: a requirement "is usually an **excerpt** … from the standard text, containing usually one specific statement", implicit requirements made explicit, a unique identifier carrying the source document, **Modality** derived from a modal-verb lexicon ("shall/must/always/never/required" versus "should/ought to" versus "may/might/permitted"), applicability conditions stated explicitly, duplicates united, relations recorded. The regex-marked modal sentences plus LLM atomisation with a verbatim quote *is* ETSI's 2013 prescription, which is the strongest available claim that the extraction stage is legitimately rule-governed and generic. One caution carries across all three families: a per-slot extractor "fills a fixed schema slot for every field **under completion pressure**; it will produce an answer regardless of whether the field is clearly present" ([arXiv 2606.24420](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24420)), which makes the verbatim-quote requirement load-bearing rather than cosmetic.

## Where the literature is silent: eight holes that are contributions, not oversights

Several of these silences are large enough to carry a paper on their own, and all of them were searched for from multiple directions rather than assumed.

**No publication matches one abstract test-purpose suite against another, and no standard or paper defines test-purpose equivalence.** ISO/IEC 9646-2's own coverage clause concerns coverage of *conformance requirements by test purposes*, not coverage of one suite by another. The conjunctive block predicate the genre licenses — same SUT, same object, same check type, same applicability condition, same expected outcome, read off TPLan's `RQ ref` + `with {}` + `when {}` + `then {}` structure — is a construction, and framing it as an operationalisation of that notation is the strongest justification available. Partial credit is equally uncited: traceability work grades the *ranking* of candidate links, never the *completeness* of a match. A defensible scheme reports strict recall (all blocks agree), core recall (applicability differs — a parameterisation defect, not a missing test, given that ETSI splits the TSS&TP per system under test into separate documents), intent recall (expected outcome differs — a diagnostic only, never coverage, because such a test would pass a non-conforming implementation), and judge-free family/clause recall.

**No published work generates a TSS&TP or an ICS proforma from natural-language standard text, and therefore no coverage figure exists for such work.** 9646-2 sanctions automated test-purpose derivation but only from a *formal* description, and declares formal-description-technique methods out of scope. ETSI's answer is model-based testing with a human modelling step. NIST's ICSGenerator builds an implementation conformance statement from an **XML instance of a formal information model**, not from prose. The LLM-era conformance generators report other quantities entirely — iPanda a **4.7×–10.8× Pass@1 improvement** ([arXiv 2507.00378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00378v1)), NeTestLLM **4632 test cases covering 41 historical FRRouting bugs against 11 by current national standards** ([arXiv 2510.13248](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13248v1)) — and none reports recall against an official standards-body suite.

**No study measures the completeness of a real conformance suite against its base standard.** Both ETSI and ISO say such a metric does not exist; what stands in for it is committee consensus. That explains the absence without substituting for it.

**No publication exists on specification-edition mismatch** between an input document and a reference suite; the nearest literature is benchmark contamination, itself a live threat here, since generated procedures in 5G testing "**significantly rely on the test case name and the components listed in the prompt**" ([arXiv 2506.10111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10111)) — which is also the reason never to let an official test's ID or title reach the judge.

**No work applies covering arrays to conformance-suite applicability predicates**, no work reports test-purpose factorisation ratios for any real suite (the TPLan authors proposed pattern mining as future work and reported no numbers, making the 118-family / 7.7-case / 112-precondition-block measurement a plausibly novel datapoint), and **no LLM work on the 9646/TPLan/TDL genre exists at all**.

**No per-row extraction accuracy is published for any ISO, ETSI or 3GPP data-element table**, and no completeness figure exists for recovering a field inventory from a document whose primary carrier is a table rather than prose or ASCII art. The count-rows-then-require-one-record-per-row pattern appears only in practitioner tooling — sec2md's report-only checks comparing each source table against its parsed output ([sec2md PR #4](https://github.com/kelab5am/sec2md/pull/4)) — never as a measured technique. Since the ingest already preserves rows as records, `objects_extracted_for_clause / rows_in_clause_tables` is a hard, annotation-free completeness oracle, directly analogous to L3X's oracle upper bound, and the observed 3-of-33 and 4-of-361 failures are detectable before any LLM is consulted. Truncation makes this urgent rather than optional: it is the "**most common production failure for schemas with array fields**", constrained decoding guarantees syntax but not completeness, and a reply that stops at the token cap "**returns partial JSON with no error raised**" ([GMI Cloud](https://www.gmicloud.ai/en/blog/structured-output-for-llm-inference-json-schema-enforcement-constrained-decoding-and-parse-failure-rates)).

**No work applies mutation testing to a specification or to a non-executable test plan** as an adequacy measure — an open slot that Masuda et al. flag as future work precisely because embedding similarity is not correctness.

One final silence is an evidence gap rather than a literature gap, and it bounds what can be claimed: **29119-4's per-technique coverage-item definitions and coverage formulae are paywalled.** The clause structure and the TD1→TD2→TD3 architecture are verified from the official free preview; what the standard says a "syntax testing coverage item" *is*, and whether its combinatorial coverage measure is expressed t-wise, are not. Annex F, "Test design technique effectiveness", would be the authoritative source for expected test counts per model element and is likewise unavailable. Acquiring the standard is the single highest-value remaining action.

## Conclusion

The project's central problem is not that a 31B model writes weak prose — the 60-case sample scoring 56 grounded, 50 actionable and 54 with a correct expected result says the opposite. It is that the tool mechanises the one step the 9646 tradition always intended to be mechanical, the clause-10.4 enumeration ladder, and omits the two steps the tradition treats as the substance of the work: ICS proforma construction and test-suite-structure design. Those two skipped steps are exactly what produces the official suite's Technology and Profile tags and its 118-family taxonomy. Thirty years ago ETR 266 reached the same verdict about MSC-based automation, finding that the two things a generated artefact kept failing to supply were **constraints** and **reference to selection criteria** — still, today, the two fields that separate an official 18013-6 record from a bare coverage item.

That reframing changes what success looks like. The defensible headline is clause coverage of the input standard, where 85–90 per cent is reachable, verifiable without the reference suite, immune to the edition mismatch, and generalisable to any standard whether or not a conformance suite has been published — paired with strict reference-suite recall as the hard secondary number, carrying its ceiling argument and its stratified denominators. On that second number a move from 13.8 per cent into the 35–55 per cent band is a credible claim; 85–90 per cent under a strict criterion is supported by no published result anywhere. The more interesting consequence is that the evaluation design is itself the novel contribution: nobody has measured a generated suite against an externally authored conformance suite, nobody has defined test-purpose equivalence, and nobody has built a non-LLM completeness oracle over a standard's own tables. A paper that reports a strict 40 per cent honestly, with the first operational definition of what matching two test purposes means, is worth considerably more than one reporting 90 per cent under a matcher nobody can defend.
